M1Q1
Ram and Lakshman were two brothers, Ram’s pocket money was twice as much as Lakshman. The good boys that Ram and Lakshman were, they never spend their pocket money on anything. They instead saved the same in their piggy bank. Every week, they would check their savings so far. Assume the first week’s savings was (R1,L1) and second week’s was (R2,L2) and so on. They try plotting their weekly savings on a graph sheet. How will the points look like?

Use GeoGebra and try to visualize how the plotted points would look over a timeline of several weeks?

Understand how to convert above given data into points on a graph?

We are basically learning the concept of converting simple real-world information into algebraic relationships (equations), which can then be represented and analyzed as points on the coordinate plane!

Let Lakshman's weekly saving be \(L\). Then Ram saves \(R = 2L\) per week
(since Ram's pocket money is twice than Lakshman, and both of them never spend their pocket money - this makes their savings stay constantly proportional).

Weekly Savings Represented as Coordinate Points

Week Ram (R) Lakshman (L) Point (R, L)
1 2 1 \((2,1)\)
2 4 2 \((4,2)\)
3 6 3 \((6,3)\)
4 8 4 \((8,4)\)
\(n\) \(2n\) \(n\) \((2n,n)\)

Observation

All points lie on the straight line:

\[ \boxed{ L=\frac{1}{2}R \qquad \text{or} \qquad R=2L } \]

The points form a straight line through the origin with slope \(\frac12\).

R = 2L
This is the equation of a straight line through the origin, so every plotted point lies on the same straight line.
M1Q2
Atul's house is centered at origin \((0,0)\) he walks straight (along the x-axis) for 2 units and then takes a left and walks 1 unit to reach Bala's house, after that he takes a right turn and walks for one unit and then a left turn and walks for one unit and reaches Chetan's house. He continues in a similar style, takes a right turn 1 unit and then left turn one unit and reaches Divya's house. Are the houses of Bala, Chetan and Divya on a straight line? What is the equation of this line? Plot this on Geogebra.

Tracing the path

Take East as the positive \(x\)-direction and North as the positive \(y\)-direction. Starting at \((0,0)\), the three houses are reached at Bala \((2,1)\), Chetan \((3,2)\), and Divya \((4,3)\).

Are the houses collinear?

\[ \operatorname{Slope}_{(2,1)\to(3,2)}=\frac{2-1}{3-2}=1, \qquad \operatorname{Slope}_{(3,2)\to(4,3)}=\frac{3-2}{4-3}=1. \]

The slopes are equal, so the three houses lie on one straight line. Using any of the points gives

\[\boxed{y=x-1 \quad\text{or}\quad x-y-1=0}.\]

M1Q4
Observe that they all pass through the origin. Why?

Each equation has the form \(y=mx\), with no constant term. Substituting \(x=0\) gives

\[y=m(0)=0.\]

Thus \((0,0)\) satisfies every equation of this form. In particular, it is a common point of \(y=x\), \(y=2x\), and \(y=10x\), so all three lines pass through the origin.

M1Q5
Plot \(y = 2x + 1\). Observe, why doesn't it pass through the origin?

Substitute \(x=0\) into the equation:

\[x=0 \implies y=2(0)+1=1\ne0.\]

Therefore, the line passes through \((0,1)\), not \((0,0)\). Its \(y\)-intercept is \(1\), and the constant term shifts the line one unit upward, parallel to \(y=2x\).

Key insight

A line \(y=ax+b\) passes through the origin exactly when \(b=0\).

M1Q6
Plot \(y = ax + b\), with \(a\) and \(b\) as parameters which you should be able to vary. What do you observe?

The two parameters affect the graph in different ways:

ParameterEffect on the line
\(a\) (slope)Changes the tilt. If \(a>0\), the line rises; if \(a<0\), it falls; and if \(a=0\), it is horizontal.
\(b\) (intercept)Moves the line vertically. Its \(y\)-intercept is \((0,b)\), and it passes through the origin exactly when \(b=0\).

Special cases

  • \(a=0,\ b\ne0\): the horizontal line \(y=b\).
  • \(b=0\): a line through the origin.
  • \(a\ne0,\ b=0\): a line through the origin with slope \(a\).

M1Q7
Consider the following simultaneous equation:
\[2x + 3y = 7\]
\[3x + 4y = 10\]
Do you see a \(2 \times 2\) matrix here? What is the importance of seeing a matrix in this problem? Why study matrices in general?

Do you observe that this problem can be retold as:
\[\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 10 \end{pmatrix}\]

Matrix form

\[ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\3&4\end{pmatrix}}_{A} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}}_{\vec{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}7\\10\end{pmatrix}}_{\vec{b}}. \]

Solving by elimination

Multiply the first equation by \(3\) and the second by \(2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} 6x+9y&=21,\\ 6x+8y&=20. \end{aligned} \]

Subtracting gives \(y=1\). Substitution into \(2x+3y=7\) then gives \(x=2\). Hence

\[\boxed{x=2,\quad y=1}.\]

Why matrices matter

Matrices encode systems of equations compactly and let the same methods scale to very large systems. They also represent linear transformations used in computer graphics, machine learning, physics, and many other fields.

M1Q8
Consider a simple function \(f(x) = 3x + 2\). This function is invertible right? Can you tell us what is \(\alpha\) such that \(f(\alpha) = 17\)? Is such an \(\alpha\) unique? How did you find such an \(\alpha\)? Is this always possible?

Set \(f(\alpha)=17\) and solve:

\[ 3\alpha+2=17 \implies 3\alpha=15 \implies \boxed{\alpha=5}. \]

Yes, the solution is unique. The function has nonzero slope, so it is one-to-one; it is also onto \(\mathbb{R}\). For any real output \(c\),

\[f(\alpha)=c \implies \alpha=\frac{c-2}{3}.\]

Equivalently, its inverse is \(f^{-1}(c)=\frac{c-2}{3}\), so every real output has exactly one real input.

Substitute \(x=5\):

\[f(5)=5^2-10=25-10=\boxed{15}.\]
M1Q10
Consider the function \(f(x) = x^2 - 10\), if \(f(\alpha) = 54\), what is \(\alpha\)?

Set the function equal to \(54\):

\[ \alpha^2-10=54 \implies \alpha^2=64 \implies \boxed{\alpha=\pm8}. \]

The solution is not unique: both \(8\) and \(-8\) give the output \(54\). This happens because the parabola is not one-to-one:

\[f(8)=f(-8)=54.\]

The function becomes invertible if its domain is restricted, for example, to \(x\ge0\) (with the corresponding range \([-10,\infty)\)).

M1Q11

Set \(g(x)=-22\):

\[ x^3-x^2-10x+2=-22 \implies x^3-x^2-10x+24=0. \]

The Rational Root Theorem gives the candidates \(\pm1,\pm2,\pm3,\pm4,\pm6,\pm8,\pm12,\pm24\), but none is a root. Let \(h(x)=x^3-x^2-10x+24\). A numerical search gives:

This cubic has one real root; its other two roots form a complex-conjugate pair.

M1Q13
Consider the function \(\phi : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2\) defined by \(\phi(x, y) = (2x + 3y, 3x + 4y)\). What has this to do with the previous question?

The function is precisely multiplication by the coefficient matrix from the simultaneous equations:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\3&4\end{pmatrix}}_{A} \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2x+3y\\3x+4y\end{pmatrix}. \]

Thus \(\phi(\vec{x})=A\vec{x}\). In particular, solving the earlier system is the same as finding an input vector for which

\[\phi(x,y)=(7,10).\]

The input \((2,1)\) works because \(\phi(2,1)=(7,10)\). This shows how a matrix encodes a linear function from \(\mathbb{R}^2\) to \(\mathbb{R}^2\).

M1Q14
Is the function \(\phi\) invertible? In the question above on matrices, we see that it is of the form \(A\vec{x} = b\). Note that we can invert the matrix, using the method that was taught to us in our high school to find out the value for the variables \(x\) and \(y\). This is one of the many applications of matrices.

For \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\),

\[\det(A)=2(4)-3(3)=8-9=-1\ne0.\]

Therefore, \(\phi\) is invertible. Its inverse matrix is

\[ \begin{aligned} A^{-1} &=\frac{1}{\det(A)}\begin{pmatrix}4&-3\\-3&2\end{pmatrix}\\ &=\begin{pmatrix}-4&3\\3&-2\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

Hence the inverse function is

\[\boxed{\phi^{-1}(u,v)=(-4u+3v,\ 3u-2v)}.\]

For example, \(\phi(2,1)=(7,10)\), and \(\phi^{-1}(7,10)=(2,1)\). Matrix inversion explicitly undoes the linear transformation and solves \(A\vec{x}=\vec{b}\).

M1Q15
We will now see matrices as functions. Instead of \(\phi\) we will write the matrix itself:
\[\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2\]

Let \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\). Viewing \(A\) as a function means that it takes a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and returns another vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\):

\[ A\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2&3\\3&4\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2x+3y\\3x+4y\end{pmatrix}. \]

Therefore, the matrix represents exactly the same function as \(\phi(x,y)=(2x+3y,3x+4y)\). For example,

\[A\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}7\\10\end{pmatrix}.\]

It is a linear function because \(A(\vec{u}+\vec{v})=A\vec{u}+A\vec{v}\) and \(A(c\vec{u})=cA\vec{u}\). Also, \(\det(A)=-1\ne0\), so this matrix function is invertible.

M1Q16
Consider the function \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2\). This matrix takes a few elements to the origin. What are those elements? Plot this using Geogebra.

Let \(B=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}\). We need all vectors sent to \((0,0)\), so solve

\[ B\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}x+2y\\2x+4y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}. \]

The second equation is twice the first, leaving the single condition \(x+2y=0\), or \(y=-x/2\). Thus

\[ \boxed{\ker(B)=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2:x+2y=0\} =\{t(2,-1):t\in\mathbb{R}\}}. \]

Examples are \((0,0)\), \((2,-1)\), \((-2,1)\), and \((4,-2)\). Every point on that line is mapped to the origin.

GeoGebra plot

Enter y = -x/2. GeoGebra displays the entire kernel as a line through the origin.

\[\det(B)=1(4)-2(2)=0,\]

confirming that \(B\) is singular and has a nontrivial kernel.

M2Q1
\(A\) is assigned \(0\), \(B:1\), \(C:2\), and so on up to \(Z:25\).

Assume you denoted every letter with a number, as given in the table above. You need to encrypt the word \(SUDARSHANA\) which stands for the numbers: \(18, 20, 3, 0, 17, 18, 7, 0, 13, 0\).

You encrypt this using a matrix given by:
\[\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\]
So \(SUDARSHANA\) will end up becoming: \(96, 134, 6, 9, 88, 123, 14, 21, 26, 39\).

Given these numbers, how will you decrypt the message and get back \(SUDARSHANA\)? This is a well known cryptographic protocol called the Hill Cipher. You can read more online.

Decrypting the Hill Cipher

For the key matrix

\[K=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\]

the determinant is

\[\det(K)=2\cdot4-3\cdot3=-1\ne0.\]

Therefore,

\[K^{-1}=\frac1{-1}\begin{pmatrix}4&-3\\-3&2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-4&3\\3&-2\end{pmatrix}.\]

Apply this inverse to each cipher pair:

Cipher pairPlain pairLetters
\((96,134)\)\((-4\cdot96+3\cdot134,\ 3\cdot96-2\cdot134)=(18,20)\)S U
\((6,9)\)\((3,0)\)D A
\((88,123)\)\((17,18)\)R S
\((14,21)\)\((7,0)\)H A
\((26,39)\)\((13,0)\)N A
\[\boxed{\text{Decrypted message: SUDARSHANA}}\]

Thus multiplying by \(K^{-1}\) reverses the encryption. In a standard Hill cipher, the arithmetic is performed modulo 26.

M2Q2
We encounter equations very often in our lives. Consider for example, the following situation at Baker's Cafe. The manager has a very important estimate to make. Mostly, visitors at his cafe happen to be families and they are often comprised of Children and/or Adults. He observes that there are 3 adults and 1 child at a table and their bill turns out to be Rs.1200/-. There is yet another table with 2 children and 1 adult and their bill comes out to be Rs.1000/-. Can the manager estimate the consumption of a Child/Adult? This is popularly called the Simultaneous Equations and we all remember from our school days, multiple ways in which these can be solved.

\[3A + 1C = 1200\]
\[1A + 2C = 1000\]

Solving the two simultaneous equations

Let \(A\) be the adult consumption and \(C\) the child consumption. The equations are

\[3A+C=1200,\qquad A+2C=1000.\]

Multiply the second equation by 3 and subtract the first:

\[3A+6C-(3A+C)=3000-1200\implies5C=1800\implies C=360.\]

Substituting into the first equation gives

\[3A+360=1200\implies A=280.\]
\[\boxed{A=280,\qquad C=360}.\]

Check: \(3(280)+360=1200\) and \(280+2(360)=1000\). Hence the estimated consumption is Rs. 280 per adult and Rs. 360 per child.

M2Q3
While we were taught the so called two variables and two unknowns, what if there were more equations than unknowns?

\[3A + 1C = 1200\]
\[1A + 2C = 1000\]
\[1A + 1C = 900\]

Why there is no exact solution

Subtract the third equation from the second:

\[ (A+2C)-(A+C)=1000-900\implies C=100.\]

Then the third equation gives \(A=800\). But this pair fails the first equation:

\[3(800)+100=2500\ne1200.\]

Therefore the three equations are inconsistent: no single pair \((A,C)\) satisfies all of them. The extra equation is incompatible with the first two, so we must seek a best-fit approximation rather than an exact solution.

M2Q4
Note that the previous question can be modelled as a matrix:

\[3A + 1C = 1200\]
\[1A + 2C = 1000\]
\[1A + 1C = 900\]

Observe this is same as:
\[\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} A \\ C \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1200 \\ 1000 \\ 900 \end{pmatrix}\]

Matrix model and least squares

The system can be written as \(X\vec{x}=\vec b\), where

\[X=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&2\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \vec{x}=\begin{pmatrix}A\\C\end{pmatrix},\qquad \vec b=\begin{pmatrix}1200\\1000\\900\end{pmatrix}.\]

Because the system is overdetermined and inconsistent, use the least-squares normal equations

\[X^TX\vec{x}=X^T\vec b,\qquad X^TX=\begin{pmatrix}11&6\\6&6\end{pmatrix},\quad X^T\vec b=\begin{pmatrix}5500\\4100\end{pmatrix}.\]

Solving these normal equations gives the best-fit estimate

\[\boxed{A\approx280,\qquad C\approx403.33}.\]

This minimizes the sum of the squared errors between the predicted and observed bills.

Use Geogebra:
Draw the vector \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\). Find out all those vectors which are perpendicular to this vector.

Perpendicular vectors

A vector \(x,y\) is perpendicular to \( (1,1) \) exactly when its dot product is zero:

\[x+y=0\]

Hence the complete solution is

\[\boxed{\{(t,-t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}=\operatorname{span}\{(1,-1)\}}.\]

GeoGebra: plot y=-x. This line through the origin is perpendicular to \( (1,1) \).

M3Q2
Do you observe that we are asking for vectors \(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\) such that,
\[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = 0\]

Null-space interpretation

The matrix equation is the scalar equation

\[\begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=x+y=0.\]

Therefore \(y=-x\), and all solutions are \(\boxed{(x,y)=t(1,-1), t\in\mathbb{R}}\). This is the kernel (null space) of the row vector \[1\;1\].

M3Q3
Use Geogebra and solve the above question with \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) replaced by \(\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}\). Use \((a, b)\) as parameters and check what happens to \((x, y)\).

General parameters

We solve

\[ax+by=0.\]

If \(b\ne0\), then \(y=-\frac{a}{b}x\), so

\[\boxed{(x,y)=t(b,-a),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}}.\]

If \(b=0\) and \(a\ne0\), then \(x=0\), giving the \(y\)-axis. In every nonzero case the solution is the line through the origin in direction \( (b,-a) \), since \( (a,b)\cdot(b,-a)=0\).

M3Q8
In the set \(W\) of previous question, find out all the points \((x, y, z)\) satisfying the following: (Use Geogebra)
\[\begin{bmatrix} w_1 & w_2 & w_3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = 0\]
where \((w_1, w_2, w_3) \in W\). Note that \(w_i\)'s are real numbers.

Vectors perpendicular to every vector in \(W\)

It is enough to be perpendicular to both basis vectors of \(W\):

\[x+2y+z=0,\qquad 2x+7y+3z=0.\]

From the first equation \(x=-2y-z\). Substitution gives \(3y+z=0\), so \(z=-3y\) and \(x=y\). Therefore

\[\boxed{W^\perp=\{t(1,1,-3):t\in\mathbb{R}\}}.\]

M6Q1
Recommender System

How do streaming platforms such as Netflix, Amazon Prime, Spotify, etc. guess which movie or song you might like next? At the heart of it lies a recommendation engine built using Matrix Factorization — a beautiful application of linear algebra on user–item rating matrices.

Think about it:
  • What does a user × movie rating matrix look like?
  • How can we fill in the missing entries using matrix factorization?
  • What role do Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) and low-rank approximation play here?

The real problem — a sparse ratings table

Imagine a streaming service with 10,000 movies and 5,000,000 users. Build a ratings matrix \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{5{,}000{,}000\times 10{,}000}\) where \(R_{ij}\) is user \(i\)'s rating of movie \(j\). More than 99% of the entries are blank, because most users have watched very few movies. The goal is to predict the missing entries and recommend, to each user, the movies with the highest predicted rating.

Hidden-feature intuition

Even though users never declare their tastes, the patterns in their ratings reveal them. For example, Alice loves action and dislikes romance; Mad Max: Furiosa has lots of action and almost no romance; so Alice is likely to rate it highly. Each user can be represented as a vector of hidden features (action, romance, …) and each movie as a vector in the same space; the predicted rating is the dot product of the two. 

            "How romantic?" ↑
                              |
                  Movie B     •
                              |
                  Alice • —→ predicted rating
                              |
                  Movie A     •
                              |
                              ↓—————→ "How action-packed?"

Matrix factorization

Pick a small rank \(k\) (say \(k=20\)) and look for two much smaller matrices

\[ U\in\mathbb{R}^{\,n\times k},\qquad V\in\mathbb{R}^{\,m\times k} \]

where row \(U_i\) holds user \(i\)'s preferences and row \(V_j\) holds movie \(j\)'s features. The predicted rating of movie \(j\) by user \(i\) is

\[ \boxed{\;\hat R_{ij}=U_i V_j^T=\sum_{a=1}^{k}U_{ia}V_{ja}\;} \]

so the entire ratings matrix is approximated by

\[ \boxed{\;R_{\,n\times m}\;\approx\;U_{\,n\times k}\,V^T_{\,k\times m}\;} \]

Instead of one huge \(n\times m\) table, we now store two small \(n\times k\) and \(m\times k\) tables.

Finding \(U\) and \(V\) — minimizing squared error

Choose \(U,V\) so that \(UV^T\) matches the known entries as closely as possible:

\[ \min_{U,V}\;\sum_{(i,j)\in\text{known}}\bigl(R_{ij}-U_i V_j^T\bigr)^2. \]

A classic approach is alternating least squares (ALS):

  1. Initialize \(U,V\) randomly.
  2. Repeat until convergence:
    1. Fix \(V\) and solve for the best \(U\).
    2. Fix \(U\) and solve for the best \(V\).

Gradient descent on the same objective also works.

Concrete example

Suppose there are two hidden features, action and romance:

UserActionRomance
Alice0.90.1
Bob0.10.9
MovieActionRomance
Mad Max0.80.1
Titanic0.00.9

Alice's predicted preference for Mad Max is

\[ U_{\text{Alice}}\cdot V_{\text{Mad Max}}=(0.9)(0.8)+(0.1)(0.1)=0.72+0.01=0.73. \]

For Titanic it is

\[ U_{\text{Alice}}\cdot V_{\text{Titanic}}=(0.9)(0.0)+(0.1)(0.9)=0.09. \]

Since \(0.73>0.09\), the system recommends Mad Max to Alice.

Strengths and limitations

StrengthsLimitations
Handles huge sparse matricesCold-start for new users and movies
Discovers hidden structureNeeds substantial data to be useful
Personalized recommendationsCan be biased toward popular items
Scales to millions of itemsCan reinforce filter bubbles

Modern systems extend this idea with bias terms, side information (genre, age, demographics), and deep-learning variants such as neural collaborative filtering.

M6Q2
Page Ranking

When you search on Google, how does it decide which web page appears first? The famous PageRank algorithm (founders Larry Page & Sergey Brin) uses the structure of the web as a huge transition matrix and computes the stationary distribution using the Markov chains you studied earlier in Module 2.

Think about it:
  • How is the web modelled as a directed graph with an associated transition matrix?
  • Why is PageRank the dominant eigenvector of this matrix?
  • How do we handle dangling nodes and the random surfer's teleport?

The real problem

The web has billions of pages. Which ones are most important? Larry Page and Sergey Brin (1996) made a circular observation precise with linear algebra:

\[\boxed{\;\text{A page is important if important pages link to it.}\;}\]

The web as a directed graph

Each page is a node; each hyperlink is a directed edge. Page \(A\) with two outgoing links, \(A\to B\) and \(A\to C\), means a visitor on \(A\) is equally likely to go to \(B\) or \(C\). A page with no outgoing links is a dangling node; a page that links only to itself is a spider trap.

The random surfer with damping

Imagine a random surfer who, on every step, with probability \(d\) (say \(d=0.85\)) clicks a random link on the current page, and with probability \(1-d\) “teleports” to a uniformly chosen page on the entire web. The PageRank of a page is the long-run probability that this surfer is on it.

The transition matrix

Build the column-stochastic matrix

\[ M_{ij}=\begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{L_j}, & \text{if page } j \text{ links to page } i,\\[6pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \]

where \(L_j\) is the number of outgoing links of page \(j\). Then the surfer's evolution is the random walk driven by \(M\). However, dangling nodes make one column all-zero (rank-deficient), and spider traps absorb the walker; we need damping to fix both problems.

The Google matrix

With damping, the Google matrix is

\[ \boxed{\;G = d\,M + \dfrac{1-d}{N}\,\mathbf 1\,\mathbf 1^{\,T}\;},\qquad G\,p=p. \]

Therefore, the PageRank vector \(p\) is precisely the dominant eigenvector of \(G\) — the eigenvector with eigenvalue \(1\).

Why we need damping

PathologyWhat happensHow damping fixes it
Dangling nodesA page with no outgoing links sinks probability until probability mass vanishes.Teleport replaces lost mass with a uniform distribution.
Spider trapsA self-link cluster absorbs all the random-walk mass.Teleport lets the walker always escape.

Google's classic choice is \(d=0.85\).

Concrete example: four pages

Take four pages \(A,B,C,D\) with links \(A\to B,C\); \(B\to D\); \(C\to A,B\); \(D\to A,C\). Then \(L_A=2,\;L_B=1,\;L_C=2,\;L_D=2\), and

\[ M = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 0 & 1/2 & 0 \\ 1/2 & 0 & 0 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

With \(d=0.85\), \(N=4\) and \(J\) the all-ones matrix, the Google matrix is

\[ G = 0.85\,M + \dfrac{0.15}{4}\,J. \]

Solving \(Gp=p\) with \(\sum_i p_i=1\) gives the steady-state

\[ \boxed{\;p \approx (0.290,\;0.197,\;0.290,\;0.224).\;} \]

So pages \(A\) and \(C\) tie for the top, \(D\) comes next, \(B\) is last.

Why PageRank converges

By the Perron–Frobenius theorem, a column-stochastic matrix whose entries are strictly positive has a unique largest eigenvalue equal to \(1\), with a strictly positive eigenvector. The damping term guarantees strict positivity, so:

  • \(G\) has spectral radius \(1\).
  • The power iteration \(p^{(k+1)}=Gp^{(k)}\) converges to \(p\) from any starting vector.

In symbols,

\[\;\lim_{k\to\infty}G^k\,p^{(0)}=p,\qquad p^{(0)}=\Bigl(\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14\Bigr).\;\]

Take-aways

  • Model: web as a directed graph; link probability = adjacency ÷ outdegree.
  • Fix pathologies: damping + teleport with probability \(1-d\).
  • Compute: dominant eigenvector of \(G=dM+\tfrac{1-d}{N}\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T\) via power iteration.
M6Q3
Dimensionality Reduction

Modern datasets (images, text, genes) live in extremely high-dimensional spaces. But the intrinsic information often lies on a much lower-dimensional manifold. Finding this lower-dimensional representation is the goal of dimensionality reduction, with Principal Component Analysis (PCA) being the workhorse.

Think about it:
  • What does PCA do? (Hint: it rotates the basis to align with directions of maximum variance.)
  • How does it use the eigen-decomposition of the covariance matrix?
  • Real-world applications: face recognition (Eigenfaces), image compression, gene-expression analysis.

The real problem — too many features

Modern data is enormous: a picture may have millions of pixels, a document thousands of words, a genome millions of base pairs, a customer hundreds of attributes. Most of this is redundant or noisy. The goal of dimensionality reduction is to

  • compress the data,
  • visualize high-dimensional data,
  • remove noise,
  • discover hidden structure.

The geometry: find the important directions

Imagine data points scattered in 3D but lying close to a 2D plane. Although the data is 3D, almost all of its information is 2D. We want to find that plane, then describe each point with 2 numbers instead of 3.

Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Every matrix \(A\) of size \(m\times n\) can be factored as

\[\boxed{\;A \;=\; U\,\Sigma\,V^T.\;}\]

Here:

  • \(U\) and \(V\) are orthogonal matrices (their columns are unit vectors perpendicular to each other),
  • \(\Sigma\) is a diagonal matrix with non-negative singular values \(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots\ge0\).

Geometrically, \(A\) first rotates by \(V^T\), then stretches along the axes by \(\Sigma\), and finally rotates again by \(U\).

The best low-rank approximation

Keep only the top \(k\) singular values:

\[\;A\;\approx\;U_k\,\Sigma_k\,V_k^T.\;\]

By the Eckart–Young theorem (1936), this is the best possible rank-\(k\) approximation to \(A\) in the least-squares sense.

FormSizeStorage
Original \(A\)\(m\times n\)\(mn\) numbers
Rank-\(k\) SVDtop \(k\) factors\(k(m+n+1)\) numbers

For \(m=n=1000\) and \(k=10\): storage drops from \(10^6\) numbers to roughly \(2\times10^4\) — a 50× compression.

The singular values \(\sigma_i\) rank the importance of each direction. If \(\sigma_1\) is huge and \(\sigma_3,\sigma_4,\ldots\) are tiny, only two dimensions carry the signal.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

PCA is SVD applied to centered data, and is equivalent to using the eigen-decomposition of the covariance matrix:

  1. Center: subtract the mean so each feature has mean \(0\).
  2. Covariance: form \(C=\tfrac{1}{n}X^TX\).
  3. Eigen: compute eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(C\).
  4. Interpret: the eigenvectors are the principal components (directions of maximum variance); the eigenvalues tell how much variance each captures.
  5. Project: project the data onto the top \(k\) eigenvectors.

Each data point ends up described by \(k\) numbers instead of \(d\) features.

Concrete example: image compression

A grayscale image is just a matrix whose entries are pixel values. Take a \(1000\times1000\) image and apply SVD:

\[\;A_{1000\times1000}=U\,\Sigma\,V^T.\;\]

Keep only the top 50 singular values:

\[\;A\;\approx\;U_{1000\times 50}\,\Sigma_{50\times 50}\,V_{1000\times 50}^T.\;\]

Storage: \(10^6\) numbers become roughly \(50\times2000=10^5\) numbers — a 10× smaller file with negligible visible difference. The discarded singular values correspond to fine details and noise that the eye barely notices.
